A lost phone is inconvenient. It becomes a business problem when the same phone holds the only way to recover your email, your bank login and the accounts that publish under your company’s name. Start with an ordinary question. If your phone went missing this afternoon, which account would you need back first?

<h2 id="the-anatomy-of-personal-information-exposure">Draw your account recovery map</h2>

Write down your primary inbox, mobile carrier, bank and domain registrar. Next to each, note where password resets go, who controls that address or number, and where you keep a backup way in. Keep the map private and leave passwords and recovery codes off the list. Look for circular dependencies, such as an inbox that can only be recovered through an old work address.

<h2 id="email-the-oldest-unlocked-door">Secure the inbox that resets everything else</h2>

Use a unique password from a password manager. Turn on multifactor authentication and set up a second recovery method offered by the provider. Store recovery codes somewhere you can reach if the phone is gone. Review signed-in devices, forwarding rules and the address that receives recovery messages.

Suppose a message says your domain renewal failed. Open your registrar through a saved bookmark and check billing there. A familiar logo or the name of a colleague is easy to copy. If someone entered their credentials on the wrong page, use the provider’s recovery process and review account activity. [FTC guide to recognizing and responding to phishing](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-avoid-phishing-scams).

<h2 id="financial--banking-security">Choose the right safeguard for the financial risk</h2>

For a charge you don’t recognize, open the bank’s app or call the number on your card. Set transaction alerts to a destination you actually monitor. Keep contact details handy outside the phone in case you lose access to both the device and its banking app.

In the United States, a credit freeze restricts access to your credit report and can help prevent someone from opening credit in your name. You place it separately with each of the three nationwide credit bureaus. Existing accounts still need monitoring. [FTC guide to credit freezes and fraud alerts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/credit-freezes-and-fraud-alerts).

<h2 id="ai-tools-the-training-data-pipeline">Decide what belongs in an AI prompt</h2>

An employee drafting a follow-up email may need the customer’s question and none of the names, signatures or account numbers in the original thread. Give the tool a fictional example or a short summary. For client work, check which workspace your organization approved and what it permits you to submit.

Training preferences, retention and link sharing are separate settings. In ChatGPT, for example, you can control whether conversations help train models. Check the controls available in the account you are actually using. [OpenAI’s guide to ChatGPT data controls](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/7730893-data-controls-in-chatgpt).

<h2 id="cell-phones--gps">Review who can see your location</h2>

Open the phone’s permission list and look for apps with ongoing location access. A map may need precise location for a journey. A shop showing nearby stores may work with a manually entered town. Change one setting, then try the feature you use. Also review who can see your location through family sharing, photo sharing and old devices.

<h2 id="defense--intelligence-agencies">Consider who else gets a copy</h2>

A service you deliberately use can pass information to another organization. Before installing a customer-tracking tool, find out what it collects, who receives it and how long they keep it. Leave fields such as personal email addresses out of page URLs that analytics services may record. [FTC overview of how websites and apps collect and use information](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-websites-and-apps-collect-and-use-your-information).

<h2 id="reputation-security">Check what a stranger can find</h2>

Search for your name alongside your business and look at the result as a customer would. Does a former address appear as the office? Is a personal number listed as the support line? Correct the original business listing first. For a people-search listing, use the site’s opt-out process and save the confirmation. A listing can reappear when its sources update. [FTC guide to people-search sites and removal requests](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-people-search-sites-sell-your-information).

<h2 id="the-defense-stack">Give each safeguard an owner</h2>

For a small team, choose who handles a lost device, a suspicious payment request and an account lockout. Keep the recovery contact for your domain and the support number for your bank somewhere the team can reach during an outage. Give each person their own account where the service supports it, so access can be removed when someone leaves.

<h2 id="building-your-personal-security-protocol">Rehearse one recovery</h2>

Pick a low-risk account and read its recovery options while you are still signed in. Check that the backup address is current and that the person responsible knows where the recovery instructions are stored. Avoid signing out of your only working session to run the exercise.

<h2 id="final-thoughts">Make the next change specific</h2>

Finish this review with one named owner and one action. “Maya will replace the former contractor’s recovery address on the domain account this week” is a task somebody can finish and verify.
